Google Tricks Search Phrases

Some Google tricks hide behind a click, a keyboard shortcut or a settings screen. The best ones hide behind nothing but a phrase. You type a few words, press Enter, and the page spins, tilts, blinks or answers with a number that makes no sense until you’ve read the right book.

This is the search-phrase list: what to type, what you’ll see, and whether it still works. It’s part of our complete list of Google Easter eggs, and the biggest tricks each get their own deep dive below.

Quick answer: A Google search trick is a phrase you type that makes the results page do something odd, like spinning, tilting or answering 42. Recent roundups list do a barrel roll, askew, anagram, recursion and the answer to life as working. Type the exact phrase, in lowercase, on a desktop browser.

What counts as a search trick?

A search trick is an Easter egg you trigger with words alone. No game panel, no extension, no special URL. It lives on the normal results page, which is both the charm and the weakness: Google can change that page whenever it likes, and a trick vanishes without any announcement.

That’s why every row below has a status. “Long-running” means it has been reported across many years. “Reported” means recent or older write-ups mention it, but we can’t promise it works on your screen today.

Visual tricks: spin, tilt and blink

These are the ones that make people laugh in meetings. Do a barrel roll spins the whole results page once, a nod to Star Fox 64. Askew leans it a few degrees to the right, and a different search puts it back, which is part of the joke. If you want the dramatic version, a floating page is a very different trick.

“Blink html” is the nerdiest of the group. It makes the word blink flash, a wink at the old HTML blink tag that browsers stopped supporting long ago.

Wordplay: the spelling-suggestion jokes

Google’s “Did you mean” line is usually a boring fix for a typo. Two tricks turn it into a punchline. Anagram suggests “nag a ram,” which is an anagram of the word itself. Recursion suggests recursion, so clicking it takes you back to the same page. If you’ve ever tried to explain recursion, you already know that’s a perfect explanation.

Numbers and the calculator

The most famous number trick lives in the calculator, not in a visual effect. Ask for the answer to life, the universe, and everything and you get 42, a joke from The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy. A blog noticed it in August 2003, which makes it one of the oldest phrase tricks still around.

Developer jokes

A handful of tricks only land if you’ve touched a web page’s source. “Blink html” and “marquee html” poke at tags that browsers dropped, and “kerning” shows you what bad letter spacing looks like. One roundup also describes a Bletchley Park trick that simulates the codebreakers decrypting the name. They’re small, quiet, and perfect for anyone who’s ever debugged a layout at midnight.

How to type a search trick

  1. Use regular Google Search on a desktop browser. Chrome is a safe choice.
  2. Type the phrase exactly, with no extra words. For the calculator trick, use lowercase and no ending punctuation.
  3. Press Enter and give the page a second to finish loading.
  4. If an effect like askew sticks, run any other search to reset it.
  5. Still nothing? Check reduced-motion settings, then try a private window.

Why a trick stops working

  • Google retired it. Zerg Rush and Atari Breakout are the famous examples.
  • The trick was tied to a promotion that ended.
  • You’re on the Google app or another results screen that skips the effect.
  • Reduced motion is switched on, which can switch off animation.
  • A redesign changed how the results page is built.
